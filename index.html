Abstract

The future will contain many new materials and devices but how will we charge them, and how will they affect the environment around us? This paper aims to identify the different new materials and to evaluate the efficiency of them so that we can understand which ones are best for us to use to charge electric cars and next-gen tech. Modern lithium-ion batteries have problems such as degradation of the battery, restrictive capacity and the reliance on raw materials which can be dealt with using various methods such as using buffer zones which includes an upper and lower limit to the battery charge but this also means that the battery will not be used to it's full capability. Batteries can be recycled or reused to mitigate raw material mining which not only is innovative but also helps the environment and reduces e-waste. One way that the new-gen will evolve is by using silicone instead of lithium-ion because "silicon electrodes can provide 10 times higher theoretical capacity and faster charging" However, silicone's volume will change by 300% during cycles which means they will degrade quickly and won’t last long (Queen Mary University of London, 2025).

Reliance on Raw Materials

The development of battery technologies have created many opportunities to improve the technosphere used in the car industry however, this development has relied on the extraction and processing of raw materials from the ground which can harm potential farmlands and habitats. Materials such as lithium, nickel and cobalt are all used significantly in the batteries and raw materials that are extracted and as the technologies develop further, acquiring these materials has become a challenge.

There are environmental, economic and ethical concerns with mineral mining. "Lithium is typically mined through a process called brine mining, which involves extracting lithium from underground saltwater reserves. The risks in polluting local water sources arise here" (Zheng, 2023). There are many examples such as the Salar de Atacama in Chile and are a growing concern for the locals living there. The next generation batteries including solid-state, sodium-ion and lithium-sulphur reduce the dependancy on these raw materials and will help but these technologies will also introduce their own limitations and requirements.

The solution to reducing material extraction is simple; we would need to invest more in material recycling, figure out different battery chemistries and we would need to change our extraction methods to be more suitable for the environment. These methods are essential and would reduce the need for additional and unnecessary mining and would help establish an appropriate battery chain.

The transition to newer technologies would need to consider and balance all factors so that the longevity of the battery and raw materials can be fully accessed and will benefit the world in the long term.

Degradation of Modern Batteries

As electric vehicles become more popular, the sustained use of their batteries can become a challenge especially in lithium-ion technology where charging and discharging repeatedly degrades the deteriorates the battery capacity which would affect the range that the vehicle can travel in one charge and overall performance.

During the charging and discharging cycle, silicon-based anodes experience massive volume fluctuations of around 300%, which frequently trigger the breakdown of the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) film (Wu et al., 2025). In severe cases, the risk of internal short circuits and a high operating temperature which can cause and accelerate unwanted chemical reactions which can produce harmful gases if not contained and will result in battery deteriation.

These limitations can be overcome by using solid state batteries because they replace the liquid chemical electrolytes with solid alternatives which can improve thermal stability and reduce degradation. "Silicon's theoretical specific capacity is so much higher (3,400–4,200 mAh/g against graphite's 372 mAh/g) which means its potential energy density is much higher too" (EV Infrastructure News, n.d.). The use of solid state alternatives is essential for extending vehicle battery lifespans, increasing the safety and durabilty of batteries and for lowering costs for material replacement.

Cost

Although developing this next generation technology will help significantly in many aspects including ecocnomic, environmental and ethical approaches, the financial cost is still a huge barrier to the redesigning of the vehicle batteries. Materials such as Lithium, Nickel, Cobalt and Graphite contribute immensely to battery production and beacause of the changes in global supply and demands, the cost of these materials fluctuates regularly. The extraction and refining process of these materials is also a lengthy and costly process which cannot be repeated without spending a large amount of money each time.

In addition, experimenting and figuring out different battery chemistries is also a costly process because it involves having various materials which can be expensive. The production of these chemistries is also tied by cost due to a complex manufacturing process with specialist materials and equipment. In a similar way, the silicon-based anodes offer a greater theoretical capacity but silicon's tendency to expand during charging and discharging can cause a greater cost to maintain a higher level of structural stability.

The economic aspect of developing batteries can be tackled by recycling materials which would reduce the dependence on newly extracted resources which would support a more sustainable and durable battery chain; this works because the money that would've been spent on extraction is not spent.

Conclusion

The development of next-generation batteries provides multiple opportunities to improve the energy density, charging speed and overall performance of electric vehicle batteries but it is also brings many problems too. Through examining the reliance on raw materials, degradation of raw materials and the cost required to produce the batteries, it is clear that certain problems and challenges must be overcome first before the technology can be implemented widely.

Although alternative chemistries can be found and could reduce reliance on raw materials, they also have their own technical limitations and requirements. Furthermore, the chemical reactions that cause battery degradation still remain an obstacle to long-term performance but can be tackled with solid-state technologies. These solid-state technologies can reduce battery degradation and improve overall battery efficiency. However, these technologies also come with a disadvantage, which is a complex manufacturing process and a greater cost.

In conclusion, next-generation batteries have the capability to transform the electric vehicle industry but they also come with certain limitations and their success in the long term will depend on balancing the development with environmental, reliability and economic aspects. Future battery research should prioritise the research and development of materials that will deliver the specifics that are required; higher energy density and faster charging capabilities while minimising degradation, reducing dependence on scarce resources and remaining affordable for large-scale production. Achieving these goals will help in the transition of more suitable green transportation.
